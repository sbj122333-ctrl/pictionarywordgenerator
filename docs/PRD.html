Summary 01

Four decisions carry this product. Everything else follows from them.

Guarantee, not heuristic

Don't build a "last 10 sessions" window. Deal the whole tier as a shuffled deck with a cursor and no word repeats until the deck is empty — 12 to 27 sessions depending on tier. Strictly stronger than the requirement, and materially simpler to build.

Difficulty is drawability

Hard does not mean obscure — obscure is just annoying. Tiers are set by a four-axis score covering concreteness, canonical form, decomposability and recognition. Obscurity is a side effect, never the goal.

Install is a P0, not a nicety

iOS Safari wipes all local storage after 7 days without a visit. A monthly party game hits that every time. Home-screen install is the only exemption, so the install prompt is the memory feature.

Corpus is capped by editors, not engineers

Every technical limit is slack — 23,700 words is 256 KB gzipped and a 2.9 KB bitmap. What binds is how many entries can pass the quality gate, and it binds hardest on God Mode.

Problem & market 02

Pictionary word generators are a commodity. A search returns a dozen free tools, several of them competent: miniwebtool already ships four tiers, a timer, difficulty-weighted team scoring and no-repeat-within-a-cycle; pictionarygen carries 2,040 prompts across 3 levels and 18 categories. Any PRD that claims tiers and non-repetition as the differentiator is wrong.

The gaps that survive contact with the incumbents are narrower and more defensible:

  • Non-repetition doesn't survive the browser closing. Competitors dedupe within a cycle held in page state. Nobody commits to a guarantee that spans sessions, weeks, and a phone that has been rebooted — which is the actual shape of party-game usage.
  • The tiers are undocumented taste. No competitor publishes what makes a word "hard". Without a rubric, tiers drift as the list grows, and "Expert" quietly becomes "words the author found unusual".
  • Volume without curation. 2,040 prompts sounds like plenty until you notice how many are filler. There is no stated quality gate, so lists pad out with near-duplicates and dull nouns.
  • Nothing works in a living room. These are ad-supported SEO pages that need a network. The game is played on a sofa with four people, patchy wifi and one phone being passed around.

So the wedge is durability and editorial standard, not feature count. That is a harder thing to copy than a fourth difficulty button.

The memory engine 04

The core requirement — no repeats across at least 10 sessions on a device — and why the obvious implementation is the wrong one.

The counter-intuitive part

A literal "no repeats within the last 10 sessions" needs per-session word lists, session boundary detection, timestamps and an eviction policy — and it still permits a repeat at session eleven. Dealing the entire tier as one shuffled deck and walking a cursor through it needs two numbers and a bitmap, and permits no repeat at all until the tier is exhausted. The stronger guarantee is the cheaper one. Build that.

// per tier, persisted
deck = seededShuffle(unseenWordIds, seed)
draw() → deck[cursor++], then seen[wordId] = 1
// the bitmap is the source of truth. the deck is a materialised view of it.

Why keep a bitmap when a cursor would do

Because the word list will change. Add 200 words, retire 50, move three from Hard to Moderate, and a cursor into an old shuffle points at nothing meaningful. The bitmap is keyed by a frozen, append-only ordinal — assigned once at authoring, never reused, never re-sorted; retired words keep their ordinal as a tombstone. On any corpus update the app recomputes unseen from the bitmap and reshuffles only that, so the guarantee survives content releases. Verified in simulation: 500 draws, then a +200/−50 corpus update, then 150 more draws — zero repeats across the boundary.

A word is spent when it is seen, not when it is guessed

The word burns at reveal, committed to storage before it paints — regardless of whether the team got it, passed, or ran out of time. The drawer has seen it; serving it to the same group again is a repeat by any definition a player would recognise. This also makes the system crash-safe: a tab closed mid-round loses one word from the pool, where burning on "correct" would leak repeats instead.

Running out

At 90% depletion the tier shows a quiet "42 words left" marker, so exhaustion is never a mid-party surprise. At 100% the app celebrates it — a deck-complete screen with the count and a badge — and offers a fresh cycle. The new shuffle applies a cooldown: the 50 most recently seen words are forced into the back half of the new deck, so a new cycle never opens with déjà vu.

Storage, and the thing that breaks it

Footprint is a non-issue, and stays one at any corpus size: even at the 23,700-word ceiling with every tier and pack exhausted, the record is 2.9 KB against a 5 MB quota. The real threat is eviction. WebKit "deletes all cookies created in JavaScript and all other script-writeable storage after 7 days of no user interaction with the website" — localStorage, IndexedDB, service worker registrations, the lot. A game played monthly trips this every single time. WebKit grants exactly one exemption: "the first-party domain of home screen web applications is exempt from ITP's 7-day cap." Hence three mitigations, all P0:

  • Install prompt after the first completed session — framed as "keep your word history", because that is literally what it does.
  • Memory Code — the bitmap compressed to a short shareable string, exportable and importable. Restores after a wipe, and moves history between browsers or devices without a server.
  • Honest state — a heartbeat key detects a wipe on load and says so plainly, offering the code, rather than silently starting over.

Difficulty system 05

The tiers have to be a defensible measure, or they drift into personal taste as the corpus grows.

Difficulty in Pictionary is not vocabulary difficulty. It is drawability. Score each candidate 1–5 on four axes; the composite sets the tier.

CConcretenessCan you point at it in the physical world? 5 = an object. 1 = a pure abstraction.
FCanonical formDoes one image come to everyone's mind? 5 = banana. 1 = freedom.
DDecomposabilityCan it be split into drawable parts or sequenced? 5 = trivially. 1 = not at all.
RRecognitionWill 9 of 10 adults in the room know the term? 5 = universal. 1 = niche.
difficulty = 20 − (C + F + D + R)
Easy 0–1  ·  Moderate 2–7  ·  above 7: Hard or God Mode by domain

Two corrections from building the corpus

The bands above are not the ones this document first carried. Authoring the 1,100-word seed corpus and running every entry through the rubric forced two changes, and both are worth stating because they are the kind of thing that quietly drifts back.

Easy is 0–1, not 0–2. A word scoring 2 has two axes below perfect, which in practice means a composed drawing rather than one canonical shape. "Vending machine" (5,4,4,5 = 2) looks like a fair Easy score and is plainly harder to draw than "cat" (5,5,5,5 = 0). Roller coaster moves to Moderate for the same reason.

The four axes cannot separate Hard from God Mode. Both tiers are abstract, so both land in 8–16 and any boundary between them is arbitrary — "peer pressure" and "the bystander effect" both score 11. What actually separates them is register: God Mode words are named terms of art from a specialist domain and need a hint; Hard words are abstract but everyday language. So above 7 the tier is decided by domain, not by score:

// specialist domains
biases · philosophy · science · biology
economics · internet · maths · literature
score > 7 and a specialist domain → God Mode. Otherwise → Hard.

This also correctly demotes drawable science. "Black hole" scores 6, "solar eclipse" 5, "photosynthesis" 7 — all Moderate, not God Mode, which is right: everyone can draw a black hole. Tier is now a pure function of score and domain, with no override mechanism at all; a word that feels wrong gets its scores or its category fixed, never a special case.

A fifth axis, Trap (T), is scored separately and does not affect difficulty — it drives fun. Trap measures how many wrong-but-close guesses the obvious drawing invites. "Dolphin" is trivially easy and scores a 5, because every room shouts "shark!" first.

The fun gate

A word that clears the difficulty rubric can still be dull. Two gates, tier-aware, because a trap is a virtue above Easy and a nuisance inside it:

  • Easy — must be instantly drawable (C ≥ 4) and universally known (R = 5). Speed and certainty are the point; ambiguity here just stalls the warm-up.
  • Moderate and above — must score T ≥ 2, or carry a documented delight property: funny to draw, satisfying to land, a groan on the reveal. Words that are merely hard and boring get cut.

Calibration: the rubric versus intuition

Tested on 19 hand-placed words, the rubric agreed with intuition on 18. The one disagreement is the useful one — and the full 1,100-word seed build later forced the two corrections above, which is exactly what a calibration corpus is for:

Corpus plan 06

The word list is the product. The code is a weekend; the corpus is the moat. Maximise it — but know which wall you hit first.

Sizing works backwards from the requirement. A heavy session runs about 50 draws (P90); a typical one about 25. God Mode sessions are short by nature — 8 to 15 — because nobody plays forty rounds of "survivorship bias". Worst case is a group that plays one tier exclusively, so each tier must independently clear ten heavy sessions.

Nothing technical limits corpus size

Worth settling first, because it removes the constraint most teams assume is binding. Authoring fields — the four axis scores, override justification, volatility, provenance — are build-time only and stripped from the shipped bundle, which carries just ord, text, words, category and (God Mode only) hint. At that density:

Every technical budget has slack even at the absolute ceiling. Tiers lazy-load, so a session downloads only the tier being played.

Where the supply actually runs out

Each tier has a different natural reservoir, and they are not the same size. This is the real ceiling:

God Mode cannot be scaled proportionally, and should not be. Push it past roughly 1,500 and you are adding terms that fail the Recognition axis — obscure rather than hard, which is precisely the trap the rubric exists to avoid. A God Mode tier at 1,500 words already supports 100 heavy sessions; it does not need parity with Easy, and forcing parity would destroy it.

Beyond the core: packs

Past 13,000, growth comes from additional corpora rather than deeper tiers. Each pack is independently sized to clear the 10-session bar on its own, because a pack that runs dry mid-party is worse than no pack.

Do not ship the bare floor. At exactly 500 words the deck runs dry on the final draw of session ten — the requirement is met and the product is broken.

And note where the genuine diminishing return sits. At the V2 ceiling a device needs 70 to 100 heavy sessions — years of monthly play — to exhaust one tier. How 13,000 curated words actually gets made

Volume at this scale is only achievable as a pipeline. Hand-authoring is the failure mode — it is slow, and it drifts. The gates are what create the value; generation is the cheap part.

1

Overgenerate against category quotas

Produce 3–5× the target per category, so the gates have something to reject. Quotas prevent a corpus that is 40% animals.

2

Mechanical filters — free, and they cut hardest

Exact and fuzzy-stem dedupe against the entire existing corpus; ban-list; word-count bounds; and a frequency check against a reference corpus as an objective proxy for the Recognition axis, which is otherwise the most subjective of the four.

3

Assisted scoring, calibrated on a golden set

Score survivors on all five axes automatically, with a human-scored golden set of ~200 words used to check calibration each run. Drift on the golden set halts the batch.

4

Human review on survivors only

By this point the batch is small and pre-sorted. The reviewer is making taste calls on the fun gate, not grinding through raw candidates — which is the only step that genuinely needs a person and the only one worth their time.

5

Playtest gate — God Mode only

Ships only if at least one of three playtest groups guessed it with the hint. Restricted to God Mode because it is the expensive gate and the only tier that needs it.

Editorial rules

  • One word, one tier. A word lives in exactly one tier, globally unique across the corpus. Otherwise the no-repeat guarantee becomes incoherent the moment someone switches tiers.
  • Near-duplicate check at build time. "ice cream" and "ice cream truck" may both exist; "cell phone" and "mobile phone" may not. Fuzzy match on normalised stems, flagged for human review.
  • Category quotas. Each tier draws across 8–12 named categories with a cap per category, so the corpus doesn't become 300 animals.
  • Anti-clustering at deal time. Look ahead 8 cards and swap to enforce: no two consecutive words from one category, and no more than 3 of any category per 20 draws. Swaps happen within the remaining deck, so the permutation — and the guarantee — is untouched.
  • Volatility tag. Every word marked evergreen or topical. Memes rot; topical entries get reviewed at each content release and pruned without ceremony.
  • Ban list. No slurs, violence, sexual content, real private individuals, or anything requiring specialist professional knowledge. Brands only where genuinely evergreen and iconic.
  • Playtest gate for God Mode. A God Mode word ships only if at least one of three playtest groups guessed it with the hint. Unguessable isn't a difficulty tier, it's a bug.
  • Locale tags. Every word tagged global or with a locale. V1.0 ships global only; an India pack and a US pack are V1.2, not an afterthought bolted onto a list that already assumes one culture.

Game loop & UX 07

One phone, passed around a room, in bad light, with people watching. That is the whole design constraint.

1

Land and pick a tier

Four tier cards, colour-coded, each showing words remaining. No sign-up, no modal, no cookie banner. Tapping a tier starts a game.

Target: first word on screen within 20 seconds of landing, two taps maximum.
2

Optional team setup

Skippable. 2–6 teams, editable names, defaults to "Team 1 / Team 2". Skipping it runs the generator with no scoring at all.

3

The privacy guard

A full-screen cover reading "Drawer only — tap when you're holding the phone". The word is never rendered behind it. This is the single most important screen in the app: a word glimpsed during the hand-off kills the round.

The word burns here, on reveal — committed to storage before it paints.
4

The word

Large type, tier colour, and word-count pips — ●● for two words, ●●● for three — so the drawer can signal the count, which is standard play. God Mode adds a "Show hint (½ points)" control.

5

Draw, with an optional twist

Timer ring: 60 / 90 / 120s or off, colour-shifting for the last ten seconds. A Twist toggle injects a random constraint into roughly one round in five — non-dominant hand, no lifting the pen, only circles, 15-second lightning.

Twists are the cheapest fun-per-word in the product: they multiply variety without consuming corpus.
6

Resolve and pass on

Three outcomes — Got it / Pass / Time's up. All three burn the word; only the first scores. Then straight back to the cover screen for the next drawer.

7

Session summary

Words played, hit rate, winning team, and the full list of words seen — so the room can relitigate the round that was robbed. First completed session triggers the install prompt.

Scope boundary 11

In for V1.0

  • ✓Four tiers with a launch corpus of 4,800 curated words
  • ✓Chunked, lazy-loaded corpus built to scale to 23,700 without re-architecture
  • ✓Cross-session non-repetition with the bitmap engine
  • ✓Memory Code export / import
  • ✓Timer, team scoring, twists, session summary
  • ✓Installable offline PWA
  • ✓English, global locale only

Explicitly out

  • ✕A drawing canvas. Pictionary is played on paper. Adding a canvas makes this a different, much larger product competing with skribbl.io.
  • ✕Accounts and server sync. The Memory Code covers the real need without a backend, a privacy policy, or a hosting bill.
  • ✕Online multiplayer. Different product, different market.
  • ✕Ads. The incumbents' business model is also their weakness. Don't inherit it.
  • ✕Other languages. Translation of idioms and God Mode concepts is authorship, not translation. Do it properly later or not at all.

Tier drift. As the corpus grows, "hard" quietly becomes "words the author found unusual".MedThe rubric is enforced in CI: computed band must match assigned tier, or carry a logged ±1 override with justification.
Commodity category, zero switching cost. A dozen free competitors rank ahead of you on search.MedCompete on the two things an SEO page can't fake — durable memory and curation quality. Installed users don't re-search.
Topical words rot. Meme-derived entries age badly and date the whole product.LowVolatility tag on every word; topical entries reviewed and pruned at each content release.

Metrics 13

An honest complication: no backend means most of these are unmeasurable at launch.

With NFR-03 as written — nothing leaves the device — there is no analytics pipeline, and success metrics become an act of faith. That trade-off should be made deliberately rather than discovered later. Three options, in preference order:

  • Recommended for V1.0 — local-only stats, surfaced to the user. The app computes its own numbers and shows them in a "your stats" view. The player gets something; the team gets nothing. Privacy stays absolute and becomes a marketing claim rather than a compromise.
  • V1.1 if measurement matters — consent-gated aggregate ping. One anonymous, opt-in event per completed session carrying tier, draw count and app version. No word IDs, no identifiers, no timestamps finer than the day.
  • Rejected — third-party analytics. Breaks NFR-03 and the offline guarantee, and hands the one genuinely differentiating claim to a vendor.
